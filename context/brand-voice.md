# Brand Voice

## Company Overview

**Industry**: Addiction and recovery
**Type**: Recovery support and coaching
**Target Audience**: People struggling with addiction, and families of people with addiction
**Focus**: All substances and behavioral addictions
**Unique Value Proposition**: Warm, human-centered recovery support that meets people where they are — without shame, without judgment, with real hope.

---

## Voice Pillars

### 1. Compassionate and Non-Judgmental
Recovery is hard. People come to us carrying shame, fear, and exhaustion. Our voice never adds to that weight. We speak to the person, not the addiction.

**Sounds like**: "If you've tried to quit before and it didn't stick, that doesn't mean you failed — it means you're human. Most people need more than one attempt before finding what works for them."

**Doesn't sound like**: "Many addicts struggle to maintain sobriety without proper support." (Avoid "addict" as a noun; avoid clinical detachment)

---

### 2. Honest Without Being Harsh
We don't sugarcoat recovery — it's one of the hardest things a person can do. But honesty in our voice is paired with warmth. We acknowledge difficulty without catastrophizing, and we tell the truth without lecturing.

**Sounds like**: "Withdrawal is genuinely difficult. Here's what it actually feels like, and here's what helps."

**Doesn't sound like**: "The dangers of addiction cannot be overstated. This disease destroys lives, families, and futures." (Avoid fear-mongering or doom framing)

---

### 3. Hopeful and Grounded in Reality
Hope is our north star — but it's earned hope, not empty cheerfulness. We share real recovery stories, real progress markers, real tools. We believe recovery is possible for everyone, and we write like we mean it.

**Sounds like**: "Recovery isn't a straight line. There will be hard days. But there are also days that surprise you — days when you realize how far you've come."

**Doesn't sound like**: "You can do it! Recovery is possible if you just believe in yourself!" (Avoid hollow positivity)

---

### 4. Accessible and Clear
Our readers are often in crisis, stressed, scared, or exhausted. They don't have the mental bandwidth for jargon, complex sentence structures, or articles that bury the point. We write plainly, directly, and with purpose.

**Sounds like**: "Naloxone (Narcan) is a medication that can reverse an opioid overdose. Here's how to get it and how to use it."

**Doesn't sound like**: "Pharmacological intervention via opioid antagonists such as naloxone represents a critical harm reduction modality..." (Avoid clinical or academic language)

---

### 5. Inclusive and Person-First
Recovery looks different for everyone. We do not assume someone's path, their beliefs, their family structure, or what "success" looks like for them. We use person-first language and write for the full spectrum of recovery journeys.

**Sounds like**: "Whether you're aiming for abstinence, cutting back, or just figuring out where to start — there's a place here for you."

**Doesn't sound like**: "For addicts and alcoholics seeking sobriety..." (Avoid stigmatizing labels)

---

## Tone by Content Type

| Content Type | Tone | Notes |
|-------------|------|-------|
| Blog posts for individuals | Warm, direct, empowering | Like advice from a trusted friend in recovery |
| Blog posts for families | Compassionate, practical, validating | Acknowledge how hard the family role is; provide concrete tools |
| Resource guides | Clear, calm, factual | Reduce overwhelm; organize information into digestible steps |
| Encouraging content | Hopeful, real, grounded | Avoid toxic positivity; acknowledge the struggle |
| Crisis/safety content | Calm, clear, action-oriented | No frills — just what to do right now |

## Core Brand Messages

1. **Recovery is possible** — for you, right now, wherever you are. Not just for people who "have their lives together." For everyone.

2. **Shame is the enemy of recovery** — we say this often and we mean it. Our content is a shame-free zone.

3. **You don't have to do this alone** — reaching out is the hardest step. We want to make it feel safe.

4. **Family members are struggling too** — we see and support the families, not just the person with addiction.

5. **Progress over perfection** — a return to use is not the end of the story. Recovery is not linear.

## Writing Style Guidelines

### Vocabulary

**Use these terms**:
- "Person with addiction" or "person struggling with addiction" (not "addict" as a noun)
- "Person with alcohol use disorder" or "person who drinks problematically" (not "alcoholic")
- "Return to use" or "relapse" (not "failure")
- "Recovery" (broad, inclusive of all paths)
- "Substance use" (clear and non-stigmatizing)
- "Support person," "loved one," or "family member" (for families)
- "Treatment" or "treatment program" (not "rehab" as shorthand — use full terms)

**Avoid these terms**:
- "Addict," "junkie," "drunk" used as nouns to describe a person
- "Clean" vs. "dirty" (as a binary for testing results or recovery status)
- "Hitting rock bottom" (implies people must suffer more before getting help)
- "Willpower problem" or "character flaw" (not how addiction works)
- "Struggling with demons" (melodramatic)
- Moralizing language: "should," "must," "you need to"
- "Abuse" as a noun (prefer "substance use disorder" or "problematic use")

### Sentence Structure
- Short to medium sentences. Vary the rhythm — pair a long sentence with a short one.
- Lead with empathy or a concrete truth before giving information.
- Use second person ("you") to speak directly to the reader.
- Ask questions to create connection: "Have you ever felt like you're the only one going through this?"
- Let a sentence stand alone when the point deserves space.

### Target Reader: Individuals

**Who they are**: Person in their 20s–50s who is aware their substance use has become a problem. May have tried to quit before. Feels shame, fear, and ambivalence about change.

**Pain points**: Fear of judgment, fear of withdrawal, not knowing where to start, losing relationships, losing control of their own life

**Goals**: Understand their options, feel less alone, take one small step forward

**How they talk**: Direct, self-aware, sometimes dark humor, real language (not clinical). They say "drinking too much" not "alcohol use disorder."

### Target Reader: Families

**Who they are**: Parent, spouse, sibling, or close friend of someone struggling. Exhausted, worried, often guilt-ridden or angry — sometimes both at once. May have been living with this for years.

**Pain points**: Don't know how to help without enabling, fear for their loved one's safety, neglecting their own needs, feeling powerless and invisible

**Goals**: Understand addiction better, learn how to have hard conversations, find resources, feel seen and less alone themselves

**How they talk**: Emotionally honest, searching for answers, often asking "what did I do wrong?" or "why won't they just stop?"

## Brand Personality

If our brand were a person, they would be:
- **A friend who has been through it** — knows the real experience, talks to you straight, doesn't judge
- **A calm presence in a storm** — not panicked, not preachy, just there with honest guidance
- **Knowledgeable but not superior** — shares what they know without making you feel small
- **Quietly hopeful** — doesn't force optimism, but genuinely believes in people even when they don't

They would NOT be:
- A clinical professional maintaining emotional distance
- A motivational speaker throwing hollow affirmations
- A moralizer or a scold
- Someone who makes recovery sound easy
- Someone who defines you by your worst moments

---

## Language Sensitivity Notes

This space requires extra care. Every piece of content should be considered from the perspective of:
- Someone who is in acute pain or considering reaching out for the first time
- Someone who has relapsed and feels like a complete failure
- A parent who is terrified they're about to lose their child
- Someone who has been rejected, shamed, or failed by previous treatment

When in doubt: be kinder. Be clearer. Lead with the person's humanity, not their struggle.

**Crisis resources** should be included in relevant content:
- **988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline**: Call or text 988
- **SAMHSA National Helpline**: 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7)
