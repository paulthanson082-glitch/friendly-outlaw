## Style Guidelines: What All Great Articles Do

### Introduction Pattern
Every article opens with one of these approaches:
- **A relatable moment**: Put the reader in a scene they recognize ("It usually starts the same way...")
- **A reframe**: Challenge a common assumption ("Most people think recovery means...")
- **An honest question**: Ask what the reader is actually thinking ("Have you been wondering if you have a drinking problem but aren't sure how to tell?")
- **A stat + human context**: Lead with a real number, immediately humanized ("Every day in the U.S., about 300 people die from an overdose. Behind each of those numbers is a family.")

The first paragraph **never** starts with the topic directly. It starts with the person.

### Body Content Pattern
- Each H2 section opens with a clear, simple statement of what's in that section
- Use real, specific examples (not hypotheticals) wherever possible
- Short paragraphs: 2–4 sentences max, then a line break
- One idea per paragraph
- Lists are used for steps, options, or checklists — not as a way to pad word count
- Every section earns its place: if it doesn't help the reader, cut it

### Conclusion Pattern
- Acknowledge the difficulty without minimizing it
- End with one concrete next step the reader can take right now
- Include a warm, direct invitation to reach out or keep reading
- Crisis resources where relevant

## Example 1: Individual-Focused Article

**Topic**: How to tell if your drinking has become a problem
**Audience**: Person questioning their own relationship with alcohol
**Key elements**: Non-judgmental opening, self-assessment framing, practical tools, shame-free language

---

**Example Introduction (target this style)**:

Most people don't wake up one day and decide they have a drinking problem. It's more gradual than that.

Maybe you've noticed that one glass has quietly become three. Or you've started planning your week around when you can drink. Or you said you wouldn't drink tonight — and then you did — and you're not sure why.

If any of that sounds familiar, you're probably not here by accident.

This isn't an article that's going to tell you what to do. It's not going to lecture you or give you a list of things you're doing wrong. What it will do is help you get clearer on what's actually happening, so you can decide what, if anything, you want to do about it.

---

**Example Body Section (target this style)**:

### The difference between drinking a lot and having a disorder

This is a distinction that matters, because the two are often confused.

Drinking heavily — even regularly — isn't the same as having an alcohol use disorder. A disorder, as mental health professionals define it, involves a pattern of drinking that causes real problems in your life and that you find difficult or impossible to control, even when you want to.

The clinical checklist includes things like:

- Drinking more than you intend to, more often than you intend to
- Spending a lot of time drinking, recovering from drinking, or thinking about drinking
- Finding that drinking gets in the way of work, relationships, or the things you care about
- Trying to cut back and not being able to
- Continuing to drink even when it's clearly making your life worse

If you're checking two or three of those boxes, that's worth paying attention to — not because it means you're broken, but because it means this is harder to manage than a simple choice.

---

**Example Conclusion (target this style)**:

You don't have to have it figured out to take a next step.

If something in this article resonated — even a little — that's information worth sitting with. You don't have to commit to anything. You don't have to call anyone today.

But if you're ready to talk to someone, the SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day. No judgment. No pressure. Just a real person on the other end of the line.

---

**Notes on what makes this work**:
- Opens in the reader's experience, not the topic
- "You're probably not here by accident" — acknowledges why they're reading
- Explicitly says "not going to lecture you" — directly disarms the reader's defenses
- Clinical criteria presented as a checklist (practical), with immediate reframe ("not because you're broken")
- Short paragraphs throughout — easy to read when you're already stressed
- Conclusion gives permission to not act immediately, then offers a clear low-stakes step

## Example 2: Family-Focused Article

**Topic**: How to talk to someone you love about their addiction
**Audience**: Family member wanting to have a difficult conversation
**Key elements**: Validates the family's experience first, practical but emotionally grounded, no blame

---

**Example Introduction (target this style)**:

You've been putting this conversation off for weeks. Maybe months.

You've rehearsed it in your head. You've thought about what you'd say, how they'd react, what you'd do if they got angry or denied everything. And every time you get close to saying something, something stops you.

That hesitation makes sense. You love them. You don't want to push them away. You're scared that the conversation will make things worse, not better.

Here's what I want you to know before we get into the how: there's no perfect script for this. But there are ways to have this conversation that are more likely to open a door than slam one shut.

---

**Example Body Section (target this style)**:

### Start with what you've seen, not what you feel they've done

The biggest mistake people make in these conversations is leading with accusations or ultimatums. Even if they're true. Even if you've earned the right to be angry.

When someone feels attacked, they defend themselves. That's just human nature. And defensiveness is the enemy of the conversation you're trying to have.

Instead, try leading with what you've personally observed and how it's affected you — not what they've done wrong.

Compare these two openings:

**More likely to escalate**: "You've been drinking every night and it's destroying our family."

**More likely to open dialogue**: "I've noticed you've been drinking more lately, and I've been worried. I miss you. I want to understand what's going on."

Same situation. Completely different energy. One puts them on trial. The other invites them into a conversation.

---

**Example Conclusion (target this style)**:

Whatever happens in that conversation, remember: you can't want recovery for someone more than they want it for themselves. Your job isn't to fix them. It's to make sure they know they're not alone — and that you love them enough to have the hard conversation.

That matters more than you know.

If you're not sure where to go from here, Al-Anon (al-anon.org) is a free support group specifically for families and friends of people with addiction. You don't have to go through this alone either.

---

**Notes on what makes this work**:
- "You've been putting this conversation off" — starts in their exact experience
- Validates their hesitation before offering any advice
- Acknowledges imperfection upfront: "no perfect script"
- Concrete comparison (bad vs. better opening) makes abstract advice tangible
- Conclusion gently shifts focus to the family member's own needs
- Ends with a resource for families, not just for the person with addiction

## Example 3: Resource/How-To Article

**Topic**: How to find a treatment program that's right for you
**Audience**: Person ready to explore treatment options but overwhelmed by choices
**Key elements**: Clear structure, reduces overwhelm, practical and actionable, non-pushy

---

**Example Introduction (target this style)**:

Deciding you want help is hard. What comes next can feel even harder.

A quick search for "treatment programs" returns hundreds of options with little guidance on how to tell the difference between them — or which one might actually work for you.

This guide is designed to cut through that. By the end, you'll know the main types of treatment, what questions to ask, and how to take the first step without having to figure it all out at once.

---

**Example Body Section (target this style)**:

### Outpatient vs. inpatient: what's the actual difference?

The biggest distinction in treatment is whether you live at the facility (inpatient or residential) or attend sessions while continuing to live at home (outpatient).

**Inpatient treatment** is typically the right fit when:
- You've tried outpatient before and it hasn't worked
- Your home environment makes recovery difficult or unsafe
- You're experiencing withdrawal symptoms that require medical supervision
- You need a complete break from your current circumstances to focus on recovery

**Outpatient treatment** may be a better fit when:
- You have work, school, or family responsibilities you can't step away from
- You have a stable and supportive home environment
- Your substance use is less severe or you're earlier in the process
- You've completed inpatient and need ongoing support

Neither is objectively better. The right one is the one that fits your life and gives you the best chance of sticking with it.

---

**Notes on what makes this work**:
- "Deciding you want help is hard. What comes next can feel even harder." — validates then pivots to solutions
- Promise in the intro: by the end you'll know X, Y, Z — gives readers a reason to keep reading
- Comparison table replaced with narrative + bullets (more readable, less clinical)
- Each option section explicitly says when it's the right fit (not "better" or "worse")
- "The right one is the one that fits your life" — person-centered, not prescriptive

## Patterns Across All Examples

| Element | How We Do It |
|---------|-------------|
| **Opening** | Reader's experience first. Topic second. |
| **Tone** | Like a knowledgeable friend, not a clinician |
| **Judgment** | Zero. Never implied. Never present. |
| **Complexity** | Explained simply, without being condescending |
| **Sentences** | Short. Varied. White space between thoughts. |
| **Lists** | Used for practical content; never to fill space |
| **Conclusions** | Acknowledge difficulty → one next step → resource or invitation |
| **Person-first language** | Always. "Person with addiction," not "addict." |
| **Hope** | Present throughout, but always earned and real |
| **Crisis resources** | Included wherever relevant without making content feel clinical |
